# Learning

Used in step 1.4 (the map), in the sample module and in Phase 2 (writing). These are the teaching rules with the strongest evidence behind them, each tied to a line of the map or to the writing, so they change what the lesson does as well as how it sounds. They hold for every kind of lesson; the case file adapts them.

## The map's lines

- **Start from what they bring.** The lesson opens by saying why it matters to these learners, in their terms, and what they will be able to do at the end. Each module opens with a **recall**: a question that makes them retrieve the previous module or something they already know that the new idea builds on. Recalling is the practice; a recap read aloud gives none.
- **Small steps, one new idea at a time.** A module carries one key idea. The terms it needs are defined before they are used. A module that needs three new terms at once is two modules, or the extra terms go to an earlier one.
- **Worked example before practice.** Each module shows the idea solved, step by step, before the learner tries it. A concept without a concrete example is not taught yet.
- **The level decides the scaffold.** For beginners: a worked example, then guided practice (together, with hints, in pairs), then practice alone. For learners who already know the ground, the step-by-step gets in the way: go straight to a problem and show the solution after. The practice line of each module says which steps it uses, and the last module's practice is always alone.
- **Tackle the mistake they already make.** Each module names the misconception it takes apart, shows why it is tempting and where it fails. Presenting only the right version leaves the wrong one in place.
- **Check during the lesson, at every module.** Each module has a **hinge question**: one question, usually multiple choice, asked to everyone at the point where the lesson can move on or go back. Each wrong answer is plausible and reveals a specific mistake, written next to it ("a → still adding the rate month by month"), so whoever teaches knows what to fix. A wrong answer that reveals nothing is replaced.
- **Make them retrieve.** Questions and exercises ask the learner to produce or apply ("what happens to the debt in month 3?"), not to recognize a phrase they just read. Mix in an earlier module's idea in later practice, so learners have to choose which idea applies.
- **Feedback says why.** Every exercise has its answer, and the answer says why each wrong option is wrong.
- **Practise close to real use.** The final task is a case like the one they will meet, different from the examples they saw, with the tools they will really have.
- **Spread it out.** The close leaves three to five questions in the support material for the learner to answer a few days later, without the material open.

## Load
- One term per concept, the same every time.
- The explanation and the example it refers to sit together: never "see the table two pages back", never a diagram whose labels are only in the narration.
- Picture and words together help when each does part of the work (a diagram with short labels, a screenshot with numbered steps); a picture that only decorates gets cut.
- What is interesting but doesn't serve the objective goes to the support material.

## Time
- Live: plan for the minutes to run about 20% over; a module that only fits if everything goes right doesn't fit.
- At least a third of a live lesson is the learners doing something, more in a workshop.
- Self-study: shorter modules, each with its own check, because no one is there to notice the learner got lost.

## Myths the skill doesn't follow
When the user or the material asks for one of these, say in one line that the evidence doesn't support it and offer what does.
- **Learning styles** (visual, auditory, kinesthetic learners, and matching the lesson to each): there is no adequate evidence that matching helps. Using picture and words together helps every learner.
- **The learning pyramid** or "cone of learning" with retention percentages ("we remember 10% of what we read, 90% of what we teach"): the numbers have no research behind them. That active practice beats passive listening is well supported, and the lesson says that instead.
- **Rereading and highlighting as study advice:** they rate low. Practice tests and spreading study over time rate high.

## Basis
For maintenance, not for the user. Wiggins and McTighe, *Understanding by Design* (backward design); Rosenshine, "Principles of Instruction", *American Educator*, 2012; Sweller on cognitive load and the worked-example effect; Kalyuga et al. on the expertise reversal effect; Roediger and Karpicke, 2006, on retrieval practice; Dunlosky et al., 2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, on study techniques; Black and Wiliam, 1998, and Wiliam's *Embedded Formative Assessment* on hinge questions; Mayer on multimedia learning; Keller's ARCS model on relevance; Pashler et al., 2008, on learning styles; Lalley and Miller, 2007, on the learning pyramid.
