# Visuals and charts

Used in steps 2.1 and 2.2, in the express proposal and in the rendered review. The principle: **the visual is chosen by the title's message**, not by the shape of the data. First ask "what does this slide claim?", then "which comparison proves it, and which visual shows that comparison fastest?".

## The glance test
The audience should get each slide's point in about three seconds. One point per slide, the claim in the title, and contrast, size and white space spent on what the title claims. If a stranger can't say what the slide claims after a glance, the slide is redone, not decorated.

## From message to visual

| The title's message is about… | Recommended visual | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Comparing items or categories | Sorted horizontal bars | Pie, bars in alphabetical order |
| Change over time | Line, with annotated events | Bars for long series |
| Result vs. target | Bar with a target line, bullet chart | Speedometer |
| Part of a whole | Simple or 100% stacked bar | Pie with more than 3 slices, 3D donut |
| Breaking down a change | Waterfall | Several loose bars |
| Conversion between stages | Funnel or bars by stage | — |
| Distribution | Histogram, boxplot | Average alone |
| Relationship between two variables | Scatter | Two lines on dual axes |
| Comparing segments across several metrics | Small multiples, heatmap | One chart with 8 series |
| One number that matters | Big number with context (vs. what) | A loose number |
| Process or sequence | Diagram with steps | Bullet list |
| Comparing options | Criteria table, 2x2 matrix | Running text |
| Voice of the customer | Quote + profile of who said it | Wall of quotes |
| Schedule | Timeline or simplified Gantt | Table of dates |

## Building rules
- **Highlight only what the title claims.** One highlight color for the figure in the message; the rest in gray or a neutral tone.
- **Direct labels** on bars and lines, instead of a separate legend.
- **Bars start at zero.** Lines may not, if the change is the point.
- **Less ink:** no 3D, no shadows, no heavy grid, no unnecessary borders, no logo or footer repeated on every slide unless the template requires it.
- **Round:** only the precision the audience needs.
- **Source and period** in the footer of every chart with data.
- **One chart per slide** by default; two only when the comparison between them is the message.
- **Consistency:** the same color means the same thing across the whole deck.
- **No decorative images or icons.** An image earns its place by proving the title or making the memorable moment; otherwise it is a seductive detail and goes.

## Layout by the slide's job
A deck where every slide has the same layout — a headline, three rounded cards side by side, an icon in each — reads as generated and makes every point look equally important. Choose the layout by what the slide does: a single number for a reveal, a chart for evidence, a comparison for a choice, a sequence for a process, a full image for the moment, a short ask for the close. Vary deliberately, within the visual concept.

## Density by format
- **Live:** little text; the presenter tells the story. On screen, only short key phrases, never the sentences the presenter will say: reading and listening to the same words at once hurts understanding. Speaker notes carry the detail; type large enough for the back of the room.
- **Reading:** the slide explains itself — assertion title, visual, one or two sentences reading the chart. More density is fine, with a clear hierarchy.
- **Hybrid:** slides designed for the room; speaker notes written as full reading text for whoever gets the file later. If the deck will circulate beyond the room, put a one-slide annotated summary at the front of the appendix.

## Identity materials to ask for
- Brand manual (colors, typography, logo use).
- Official template (a slides file or a template in the app they use).
- Decks previously approved by the same audience or client.
- Chart or dataviz guide, if one exists.
- Mandatory elements and constraints: logos, disclaimers, forbidden terms, rules for images.

From the material received, extract and confirm: palette (with highlight color and neutrals), typography, chart standard, mandatory elements.

## No identity material
Build it together:
1. In a single round: is the tone sober or expressive? Any known brand colors, even without a manual? A presentation they liked as a reference?
2. Propose two or three short directions, each with palette (background, text, highlight, neutrals), a type pairing and one sentence on the feel.
3. Settle on one and record it as global rules.
