# Hook, memorable moment and close

Used in step 1.2 and in the express proposal. Attention is planned; it doesn't appear on its own during the build. People judge an experience largely by its most intense moment and its end, so the moment and the close get as much care as the opening.

## Hook
How the first 30 seconds — or the first slide of a sent deck — create a tension that only the central idea resolves.
- Types: a number that contradicts what the audience believes; the question they already bring into the room; a concrete case (a customer, a situation); the cost of not acting; the big change in the world that makes the status quo untenable.
- The hook leads to the central idea; a loose trick grabs attention and then wastes it.
- In an answer-first deck, the hook is the answer itself, stated with what is at stake.

## Memorable moment
The one thing the audience will repeat the next day, placed at the turning point of the arc.
- Types: a dramatization or demonstration; a short line built to be repeated; an evocative image or a metaphor that becomes one (Phase 2 gives it form); a real story with a person in it; a striking figure revealed alone, with its comparison.
- Test: would someone tell a colleague about it in the hallway?
- Pair every key number with a concrete case; people remember stories far more than statistics.

## Close
The last content slide is the ask or the concrete action — who does what, by when — and, when possible, returns to the hook to close the circle. "Thank you" or "Questions?" is never the last content slide.

## Opening and rhythm
- The deck doesn't open with an agenda: the first content slide is the hook or the answer. In a long deck, a short map can come right after the hook, and visible section breaks help the audience follow.
- For an audience new to the subject, define the two or three terms the deck depends on early, before they're needed.
- Don't stack more than three dense slides in a row; interleave a breather (a number, a sentence, an image, a quote).

## Cut seductive details
An anecdote, image, joke or fact that is interesting but doesn't serve the central idea lowers understanding, more so for audiences new to the subject. Every element that doesn't prove a title or build the arc is cut or moved to the appendix, however good it is.
