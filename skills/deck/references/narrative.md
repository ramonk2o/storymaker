# Narrative

Used in step 1.2 and in the express proposal.

## First decision: answer first or build tension

The sources that matter disagree, and the right choice depends on the room, so decide it explicitly with the user.

- **Answer first** when the audience is deciding, is short on time or will read the deck alone (executives, committees, boards, sent decks). The first content slide states the conclusion or the ask; the rest supports it. Prepare as if the slot were cut to five minutes.
- **Build tension** when the goal is to persuade, mobilize or inspire and there is time (a talk, a change effort, a launch, a room that doesn't yet feel the problem). The deck opens a gap and resolves it toward the end.
- **Mixed:** answer first to the decision-maker, with tension inside the body (each section opens a question and answers it). Common in hybrid decks.

Whatever the choice, the central idea is never hidden from the person who decides: a tension arc for executives still states the ask before the time runs out.

## Arcs

Propose two or three that fit the decision above, with pros and cons, and recommend one.

### Answer first (pyramid)
**Structure:** conclusion or recommendation → reasons → evidence for each. Group the reasons so they are the same kind, don't overlap and leave no gap; each higher point summarizes the insight of the points below it, not just their topic.
**Use:** decision-makers, sent decks, an audience that trusts the presenter.
**Avoid:** when the conclusion will meet resistance and the audience must be led to it.

### Situation, complication, resolution (SCR / SCQA)
**Structure:** a situation everyone accepts → a complication that changes it → the question it raises → the resolution, which is the central idea.
**Use:** proposals and recommendations where the audience must understand why to change.
**Avoid:** re-explaining a situation everyone knows; start at the complication instead.

### What is vs. what could be
**Structure:** the current reality, then a turning point that opens the gap to a better future; the middle alternates between the two, raising the contrast; a second turning point is the call to action; the end describes the reward of acting.
**Use:** mobilizing, vision, culture change, launches, talks.
**Avoid:** accountability and analytical decks.

### Strategic narrative
**Structure:** name a big, undeniable change in the world → who wins and who loses because of it → the future worth reaching (a state, not features) → the obstacles → how the proposal overcomes them → evidence, ideally real cases.
**Use:** sales, strategy and positioning decks, when the audience must see why the old way stops working.
**Avoid:** opening with "you have a problem", which makes people defensive; open with the change instead.

### Problem → solution → benefit
**Structure:** a problem the audience feels → solution → what improves, for whom.
**Use:** pitches, internal selling, a tool or process.
**Avoid:** when the audience doesn't recognize the problem; prove it first.

### Before / after / bridge
**Structure:** how it is → how it could be → what takes you there.
**Use:** concrete transformations with visual proof.
**Avoid:** when the "after" is abstract or has no evidence.

### Guiding questions
**Structure:** each section answers a question the audience brings ("Did it work?", "Why?", "What now?").
**Use:** commissioned research, audiences with clear doubts.
**Avoid:** when the questions don't follow an order of importance.

### Chronological journey
**Structure:** what happened, in order.
**Use:** post-mortems, history needed to understand a decision, a customer journey.
**Avoid:** as the main structure of a results deck; chronology is rarely the order of importance.

## Checks on any arc

- **The audience is the hero.** The presenter or the company is the guide. An arc that reads as "our journey" or "what we did" is rebuilt around what changes for the audience.
- **Objection beat.** For a skeptical room, plan one moment that raises the strongest objection and refutes it with evidence. Mentioning an objection without refuting it persuades less than not mentioning it at all.
- **The middle keeps contrast.** Long runs of slides that only add more of the same lose the room; alternate problem and answer, current and possible, number and case.
- **The ask follows from the shift.** The close asks for what the audience should believe or do after the deck, as recorded in the Opening.
