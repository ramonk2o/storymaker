# Case: idea pitch

Decks that sell something to whoever decides: a project, a budget, a tool, a process change, a creative idea. Unlike a strategy proposal, a pitch is shorter and more focused on **convincing and getting a yes**.

## What the audience needs to leave knowing
- What real problem the idea solves, and how much not solving it costs.
- Why this idea works (proof, not promise).
- Exactly what is being asked for.

## Diagnosis questions
- What are you asking for, concretely? A yes to what?
- Who decides, and what does that person gain or risk by saying yes?
- What are the most likely objections? Who has already said no to something similar?
- What is the cost of not doing it?
- What proof do you have: a pilot, a test, a benchmark, another company's case, internal data?
- What is the smallest first step they can approve?

## How to phrase the central idea
Problem + idea + ask.
- Weak: "We want to present a new automation tool."
- Strong: "Automating segmentation frees up 2 days a week for the CRM team — we are asking for X for a 60-day test."

## Recommended arcs
- **Problem → cost of not acting → idea → proof → ask** (default).
- **What is vs. what could be**, for ideas that require a change of vision.
- **Before / after / bridge**, when the transformation can be shown concretely.
- **Strategic narrative**, when the case rests on a change in the market or the business that makes the current way untenable.

## Variant: investor pitch
A pitch to investors, often sent before or instead of a meeting. Investors spend only a few minutes on a sent deck, so every slide must pass the glance test and the story must hold in the titles alone.
- Typical sections: purpose, problem, solution, why now, market, competition, product, business model, traction, team, the raise and its use. Sources differ on where the team goes; put it early when the team is the main proof.
- The ask is the amount, what it buys and the milestone it reaches.
- Proof is traction: real numbers with their period, never projections presented as results.
- Short: about 10 to 15 slides, one idea each; detail goes to an appendix or a data room.

## Typical skeleton
1. The ask, in one sentence
2. The problem, as the audience feels it
3. The cost of not acting
4. The idea
5. The proof
6. Answers to the main objections
7. The detailed ask and the first step
Appendix: cost, operations and schedule details.

## Pitfalls
- Starting with the solution before the audience feels the problem.
- A vague ask, or one hidden on the last slide.
- Ignoring the obvious objection — the decision-maker will raise it anyway.
- Too much enthusiasm and too little proof.
- Asking for everything at once when a pilot would be approved more easily.

## Typical visuals
Big number for the cost of the problem, before/after, mockup or concrete example, pilot result, investment vs. return table.

## Specific stress test
- What is the first objection the decision-maker will raise, and which slide answers it?
- If they can only approve half, which half do you want?
- Why hasn't anyone done this before?
