# Case: talk

Presentations meant to move a room rather than inform a decision: a keynote, an all-hands, a kickoff, a vision or culture talk, a conference talk, a launch to a team or partners. The audience wants to **feel why this matters, believe it can change, and know their part in it**.

## What the audience needs to leave knowing
- The idea worth carrying out of the room, in one sentence they could repeat.
- Why it matters to them, not only to the presenter or the company.
- What they can do differently, starting now.

## Diagnosis questions
- Why is this audience here — by choice or obligation? What is on their mind this week?
- What do they believe or do today that the talk should change?
- What is the one idea they should be able to repeat tomorrow?
- What story, person or moment from real life carries that idea?
- What would make them resist it: fatigue with initiatives, distrust, fear of losing something?
- How long is the slot, and what comes before and after it?
- What should they do on Monday?

## How to phrase the central idea
Idea + why it matters to them + what changes.
- Weak: "Our vision for next year."
- Strong: "Customers stopped comparing us with competitors and started comparing us with the best app on their phone — next year every team wins or loses on how simple we are, and that starts with what each of us ships this quarter."

## Recommended arcs
- **What is vs. what could be** (default): tension built between the current reality and the possible future, up to the call to action.
- **Strategic narrative**, when a change in the world is what makes the current way untenable.
- **Before / after / bridge**, for a concrete transformation.

## Typical skeleton
1. The hook: a story, a change in the world or a striking contrast
2. What is: the current reality, as the audience lives it
3. The turning point: why the current way stops working
4. What could be: the future worth reaching
5. Alternating proof: real cases, people, numbers with their comparison
6. The memorable moment: the line, image or demonstration people will repeat
7. The call to action: what each person does, starting when
8. The reward: what changes for them when it works, returning to the hook

## Pitfalls
- The company or the presenter as the hero; the audience must be.
- A talk built from slides of data with no person or story in it.
- A long preamble about the presenter or the agenda before the hook.
- Too many ideas — a talk that says five things is remembered for none.
- A vague call to action ("let's embrace change").
- Dense slides the presenter reads aloud.

## Typical visuals
Full-screen images, a single word or number per slide, a real photo of a customer or a team, a before/after, a short line set large for the memorable moment. Speaker notes carry the story; the slides carry the emotion and the proof.

## Specific stress test
- What would someone tell a colleague about this talk the next day?
- Which part would a skeptical veteran of past initiatives roll their eyes at, and how does the talk earn their trust?
- If the slot is cut to five minutes, which story stays?
