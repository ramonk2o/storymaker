# Case: training

Decks for teaching: onboarding, training on a tool or process, a class, a workshop, upskilling a team or a client. The goal is not to inform, it is to make the person **able to do something afterwards**.

## When the lesson skill did the content
If the deck starts from a lesson file, with or without its approved map, the content is settled. **The lesson is the deck's content; the deck decides only how to show it.** It doesn't reopen the objective, the cut, the modules or their order, and adds no content: the central idea is the lesson's one thing, with no alternative; the hook and the memorable moment are the lesson's opening and its one thing. What the deck finds wrong or not doable in the lesson (a figure that doesn't match its source, a checked calculation or another part of the lesson; a task learners can't do with what it allows) goes to the user as a labeled proposal, with each version and where it comes from: the deck neither fixes it alone nor uses it silently. So does what the cold reader finds that would change the lesson's content. If the content isn't settled and the session can load the lesson skill, the deck isn't offered: the lesson skill plans the content first. If the session can't, this case does the whole job.

**Mode and first reply.** Recommend express mode unless the slides face clients or the public, since the lesson already passed a gate. In express, the first reply is the Proposal, in place of the Opening: its first line carries the mode recommendation and what the lesson says of the audience, and the questions the lesson leaves open (who presents, the template) join its round, five questions at most in all, the stress questions first. Its Central idea line has no alternative, its Arc line reads "the lesson's modules, in the lesson's order", and its Speaker notes line names the sources below. In full mode, the Opening asks only what the lesson leaves open, and Phase 1 skips 1.1, which the lesson settled, and runs 1.2 with the arc fixed. In both modes the stress test asks only about the slides, and what the deck finds wrong in the lesson comes before the round, as proposals A, B…, outside its numbering. The slide count is what the lesson yields, not one slide per one to two minutes.

**From lesson to slides.** What the learner sees in the lesson's body becomes slides, in the lesson's order; the header isn't shown as it is: "Why this matters" opens the deck with the hook, the objective is the slide after it, and the one thing is the memorable moment, on the slide that states it; what the teacher blocks hold becomes speaker notes; each answer in the Answers section goes into the notes of the slide that asks it; support material and sources stay in the file. So the storyline's parts are the opening, each module and the close, and the key ideas are the slide titles, shortened only with the user's ok. With practice afterwards, the lesson's body is what is said: its key phrases go on the slides and the body, as written, into their speaker notes (the short notes of `references/delivery.md` are for notes the deck writes); and the one change to the lesson's order is that the recap comes before the take-home challenge, which closes the deck with its steps and, only if the lesson gives it, where to get help.

Each slide's line in the storyline names its role after the title, in both modes (in express it reads `Title — role — evidence — visual — mark`): inside a module, recall, key idea, example, mistake, question, practice or check; outside, hook, objective, why it matters, question, task, challenge or recap; so a module missing its example or its mistake shows at a glance. A question to the room is a slide of its own, marked `[question]`, a mark this case adds: the question is its title, in place of an assertion, and its answer goes in the speaker notes; it sits where the lesson's text places it; when the lesson doesn't say, a question asking learners to predict comes before the module's example and one asking them to apply comes after it. A recall or a check slide also takes its question as its title, with the answer in the speaker notes. A question that only a teacher block suggests stays in the speaker notes.

## What the audience needs to leave knowing
- What they will be able to do at the end, and why it matters to them.
- The few essential concepts, with examples.
- Where to get help afterwards.

## Diagnosis questions
- At the end, what should the person be able to do? (An action verb: set up, analyze, write…)
- What is the starting level? What do they already know and what do they probably get wrong?
- Why does this matter to them, and not only to the company?
- How much time is there, and does practice happen in the session or afterwards, on their own?
- Live, recorded or for later reference?
- What are the most common mistakes of people learning this?
- How will you know they learned it?

## How to phrase the central idea
Capability at the end + why it matters.
- Weak: "Training on the new CRM tool."
- Strong: "By the end, every analyst builds a reactivation program alone, in under 30 minutes and with no help from the tech team."

## Recommended arcs
- **Problem → concept → example → mistake → practice → recap** (default), repeated per module.
- **Task journey**, following the order in which the person will do it in real life.
- **From simple to complex**, when the concepts depend on one another.

Whatever the arc, a module uses only terms and ideas taught before it, judged by what each slide needs, not how it is phrased; a slide that needs a later one moves, or the term moves earlier, a proposal when the order was the user's.

## Time and mode
For a training without a lesson file, the length doesn't decide the mode: an internal training is express unless it meets a full-mode criterion (clients or the public, a skeptical room, many people under the user's name). Live or hybrid, about 20% of the duration is buffer, left out of the talk time and the slide count; shares of time, such as practice, are of the minutes left, and a split the user gave is shown in minutes and confirmed in the round. Of those, only talk time counts toward the slide count: each practice block and the final task get one slide, with the instructions on screen, however long they run.

## Typical skeleton
1. What you will be able to do at the end
2. Why it matters
3–N. Per module: concept → example → its common mistake → exercise or demonstration → check
N+1. Final task: learners do it once, start to finish
N+2. Recap on one screen
N+3. Where to get help / reference material
Appendix: full reference, shortcuts, FAQ.

With practice afterwards (a short, talk-like session; learners try it on their own later): open with a hook tied to the one thing they should remember; per module concept → example → common mistake, with one or two quick questions to the room in the whole deck, each a `[question]` slide inside its module; close with a recap, then a "Try it on your own" slide with the steps and where to get help.

Two practices from learning research apply here more than anywhere: define the key terms before they're needed (the order rule above), and break the content into short modules with a visible break between them.

## Pitfalls
- Too much content for the time available — better to teach 3 things well than 10 badly.
- A concept without a concrete example.
- Mistakes gathered on one slide at the end, away from the concept they misread.
- No moment of practice or check, in the session or as a challenge to try afterwards.
- Slides too dense for live delivery, or too empty for later reference.
- Using jargon the audience doesn't know yet.

## Typical visuals
Annotated screenshots, numbered step-by-step, process diagram, right vs. wrong example, recap card.

## Specific stress test
- If the person only remembers one thing tomorrow, what should it be?
- Which part are you including because it matters to you, and not to the learner?
- How do they practice this before they really need it?
