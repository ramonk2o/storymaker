# Case: training

Decks for teaching: onboarding, training on a tool or process, a class, a workshop, upskilling a team or a client. The goal is not to inform, it is to make the person **able to do something afterwards**.

## When the lesson skill did the content
If the deck starts from a lesson file and its approved Lesson map, the content is settled: don't reopen the objective, the cut or the modules. Recommend express mode unless the slides face clients or the public, since the map already passed a gate. Skip what the map settled: the central idea is the lesson's one thing, with no alternative; no triage, arc choice or diagnosis round; the hook and the memorable moment are the lesson's opening and its one thing, never new content; the stress test asks only about the slides. The storyline (or, in full mode, the Script) turns the map into slides: the opening, one part per module and the close, each a part of the storyline of its own even when the deck is short, the modules in the map's order with their slides together (contrast, breathers and the objection beat work inside a module, never moving an example, a mistake or a question into another one), with the key ideas as slide titles (shortened only with the user's ok), each module's example, mistake, practice and check as its slides, and the teacher notes as speaker notes. When practice comes afterwards, each module becomes its key idea, example and mistake, plus the quick question to the room where there is one; the lesson's body is what is said, so it becomes the slides and their speaker notes; and the take-home challenge is the closing slide, with its steps and, only if the lesson gives it, where to get help; the lesson's recap comes right before it, so the deck closes on the ask. The cold reader on the titles runs as the mode says; what it finds that would change the lesson's content is a proposal the user approves. If the content isn't settled and the session can load the lesson skill, the deck isn't offered: the lesson skill plans the content first. If the session can't, this case does the whole job.

When the lesson file answers the Opening, the first reply in express mode is the Proposal: the questions still open (who presents, the template) join its round of stress questions, five at most, each with your hypothesis. In the Proposal (or the Script), the central idea is the lesson's one thing with no alternative, the arc reads "the lesson's modules, in the map's order", and the speaker notes come from the teacher notes and, with practice afterwards, the lesson's body. In the first reading, a figure in the lesson that doesn't match its source or another part of the lesson (a hook that says "almost double" where a module gives 2.4 times) goes to the user as a labeled proposal, with both versions: the deck neither fixes it alone nor uses it silently.

Each slide's line in the storyline names its role after the title, in both modes (in express it reads `Title — role — evidence — visual — mark`): inside a module, recall, key idea, example, mistake, question, practice or check; outside, hook, objective, why it matters, task, challenge or recap; so a module missing its example or its mistake shows at a glance. A question to the room is a slide of its own, marked `[question]`, a mark this case adds: the question is its title, in place of an assertion, and its answer goes in the speaker notes; it sits inside its module where the lesson's text places it; when the lesson doesn't say, a question asking learners to predict comes before the module's example and one asking them to apply comes after it. With practice in the lesson, the final task is the slide before the recap.

## What the audience needs to leave knowing
- What they will be able to do at the end, and why it matters to them.
- The few essential concepts, with examples.
- Where to get help afterwards.

## Diagnosis questions
- At the end, what should the person be able to do? (An action verb: set up, analyze, write…)
- What is the starting level? What do they already know and what do they probably get wrong?
- Why does this matter to them, and not only to the company?
- How much time is there, and does practice happen in the session or afterwards, on their own?
- Live, recorded or for later reference?
- What are the most common mistakes of people learning this?
- How will you know they learned it?

## How to phrase the central idea
Capability at the end + why it matters.
- Weak: "Training on the new CRM tool."
- Strong: "By the end, every analyst builds a reactivation program alone, in under 30 minutes and with no help from the tech team."

## Recommended arcs
- **Problem → concept → example → mistake → practice → recap** (default), repeated per module.
- **Task journey**, following the order in which the person will do it in real life.
- **From simple to complex**, when the concepts depend on one another.

Whatever the arc, a module uses only terms and ideas taught before it; a slide that needs a later one moves, or the term moves earlier.

## Typical skeleton
1. What you will be able to do at the end
2. Why it matters
3–N. Per module: concept → example → its common mistake → exercise or demonstration → check
N+1. Final task: learners do it once, start to finish
N+2. Recap on one screen
N+3. Where to get help / reference material
Appendix: full reference, shortcuts, FAQ.

With practice afterwards (a short, talk-like session; learners try it on their own later): open with a hook tied to the one thing they should remember; per module concept → example → common mistake, with one or two quick questions to the room in the whole deck, each a `[question]` slide inside its module; close with a recap, then a "Try it on your own" slide with the steps and where to get help.

Two practices from learning research apply here more than anywhere: define the key terms before they're needed (the order rule above), and break the content into short modules with a visible break between them.

## Pitfalls
- Too much content for the time available — better to teach 3 things well than 10 badly.
- A concept without a concrete example.
- Mistakes gathered on one slide at the end, away from the concept they misread.
- No moment of practice or check, in the session or as a challenge to try afterwards.
- Slides too dense for live delivery, or too empty for later reference.
- Using jargon the audience doesn't know yet.

## Typical visuals
Annotated screenshots, numbered step-by-step, process diagram, right vs. wrong example, recap card.

## Specific stress test
- If the person only remembers one thing tomorrow, what should it be?
- Which part are you including because it matters to you, and not to the learner?
- How do they practice this before they really need it?
