# Strategy

Used in the Strategy step and for the light path's draft. The move is how the email gets the reader from where they are to what the user needs from them. Choose it from the objective, the reader's state and the stakes, never by habit, and show it on a real opening.

## Moves

- **Lead with it** — the news, the decision or the ask in the first lines, then the reasons and what happens next. The default when the reader has to act, and nearly always for bad news.
- **Build the case** — the shared facts or goal first, the conclusion after. When the reader would reject the conclusion before seeing the reasoning, and the email is short enough that they will reach it. Never as a buffer that hides bad news.
- **Acknowledge, then disagree** — the other side's point stated fairly, in their terms, before the user's own. When the reader is invested, hurt or angry.
- **Options, not a demand** — two or three acceptable paths, one recommended. When the reader's sense of control is what they will defend.
- **Anchor in a shared goal** — what both want, then how the ask serves it. When the relationship is good and the disagreement is about means.
- **Ask instead of assert** — a real question that lets the reader reach the point or supply what the user doesn't know. When the reader outranks the user or the user may be missing information. Never a rhetorical trap.
- **Draw the line** — a clear position, its reason and what happens next, without apologizing for having it. When a boundary was crossed or ambiguity has already cost something.
- **Separate and sequence** — different emails for readers with different stakes, or a conversation before the email. When one text can't serve everyone who would receive it.

## How people read

Each principle with what it asks of the email:

- **Text arrives colder than it left.** People overestimate how well tone comes through in writing: neutral reads as curt, irony as a jab. Write warmth in on purpose and keep irony out when the stakes are high.
- **Recipients usually want the bad news first.** Givers prefer to soften first; a long preamble raises the reader's anxiety and reads as maneuvering. Say it early, then the context and what happens next.
- **Reactance.** People push back when they feel their freedom threatened: orders, "you must", ultimatums. Reasons, choices and a role for the reader lower the resistance.
- **Fairness of the process.** When the outcome is bad for the reader, how it was decided (the criteria, who was heard, whether it can be reviewed) weighs almost as much as the outcome.
- **Face.** Leave the reader an honorable way to say yes: agree without admitting fault, correct course without being exposed, especially when they outrank the user or others are in copy.
- **The cost of yes.** A specific ask, an easy next step, a clear date. A vague ask gets no answer.
- **A reason helps.** An ask with its reason gets more yeses than the bare ask; the reason has to be true.
- **Endings stick.** The last lines shape how the email is remembered. End on the next step or the relationship, not on an apology or a hedge.
- **Frames change decisions.** The same fact framed as what the reader keeps or loses changes the response. Pick the frame that is true and serves the reader's decision.
- **People keep their commitments.** Recalling something the reader actually agreed to is legitimate; inventing an agreement is not.

## The test

Every move and lever passes one test: would the reader accept it if they saw the strategy behind it? It rules out false urgency or scarcity, guilt as leverage, flattery as payment, deliberate ambiguity kept for a later "I told you", an ask buried so it can't be refused, a threat dressed as information, and an apology for something the user didn't do, offered only to end the conversation. Such moves tend to be found out, and then they cost more than the email gained.

## Pitfalls

- The praise–criticism–praise sandwich by reflex: readers learn to discount the bread.
- Over-apologizing, which hands the reader the frame that the user is at fault.
- Explaining more than the reader needs, which reads as defensive.
- The move that is comfortable for the writer instead of the one that works on the reader.
