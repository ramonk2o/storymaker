# Case: taking a stand

Disagreeing with a decision or a plan, pushing back on a request, drawing a line after it was crossed, or answering an angry, unfair or hostile message. The reader wants **to know where the user stands, why, and what happens now**, and an angry reader wants first to know they were heard.

## What the reader needs to leave with
- The position, clear enough that it can't be read as agreement.
- The reason, grounded in facts or a shared goal, not in feelings about the reader.
- What the user proposes or will do next.
- For an angry reader: that their point was understood, in their terms.

## Materials that help
The thread in full; the decision or request being disputed and who made it; the facts behind the user's position; what the user wants to keep from the relationship; earlier messages where the line was set.

## Diagnosis questions
- What does the user want to happen: a change of decision, a record of disagreement, an end to the behavior?
- Is the user willing to disagree and commit if the answer stays the same?
- What in the other side's position is right?
- Who else reads it, and does that change what can be said?
- Is the user calm enough to send it today?

## How to phrase the message
The position + the reason + what the user proposes.
- Weak: "Some concerns about the new process."
- Strong: "I don't think we should move approvals to the client's team before the launch; we'd lose two days per piece in the busiest month, and I'd propose trying it in March instead."

## Typical structures
- **Acknowledge, then disagree:** their point in their terms → where the user differs and why → the proposal → what the user will do if it stays as is.
- **Draw the line:** what happened, in facts → why it isn't acceptable → what the user needs from now on → what happens if it repeats, when that's true.
- **Answer to a hostile message:** what the user takes from it that is fair → the facts that correct the rest, without heat → the next step; nothing that answers tone with tone.

## Pitfalls
- Matching the thread's anger, or quoting the reader's words back at them.
- Disagreement so hedged it reads as agreement.
- A boundary stated with an apology attached.
- Arguing every point instead of the one that matters.
- Copying people in to win.

## Sample passage
The subject line, if new, and the paragraph that states the position.
