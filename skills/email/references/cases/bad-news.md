# Case: bad news or a no

News the reader won't want: a missed deadline, a cost overrun, a rejected proposal, a cancelled project, a no to a request, a decision that goes against them. The reader wants **to know at once what happened and what it means for them, then why, and what happens next**.

## What the reader needs to leave with
- The news itself, early and unambiguous.
- What it means for them, specifically.
- How it was decided or why it happened, briefly and honestly.
- What happens next, and what, if anything, is still open.

## Materials that help
The facts and dates; what was promised before; how the decision was made and by whom; what the user can still offer; the thread where the expectation was set.

## Diagnosis questions
- Is it decided, or still open? Is it the user's call?
- What did the reader expect, and when did they expect it?
- What can the user offer: an alternative, a new date, a partial yes?
- Is anyone else affected who should hear it first, or in person?
- How much of the cause is the user's, and does the email own it?

## How to phrase the message
The news + what it means for the reader, in one sentence.
- Weak: "An update on the project timeline."
- Strong: "We'll deliver on the 20th, not the 10th, because the API access came three weeks late; the launch can keep its date if we cut the reports module from phase one."

## Typical structures
- **Lead with it:** the news → what it means for the reader → why → what happens next → the ask, if any.
- **A no with an alternative:** the no → the reason → what the user can do instead → the next step.
- **A decision against the reader:** the decision → how it was made → what doesn't change for them → who to talk to.

## Pitfalls
- A buffer of context before the news.
- A reason that blames the reader or a third party when the user shares the cause.
- A "maybe" that is really a no, which buys a second disappointment later.
- An alternative the user can't actually deliver.
- Over-apologizing, which makes the email about the writer.
