# Case: an apology

Owning a mistake: a missed commitment, an error that cost the reader something, something said badly. The reader wants **to see that the user understands what went wrong and what it cost them, and to know it is fixed or won't happen again**.

## What the reader needs to leave with
- What went wrong, named specifically, with the user's part owned.
- The impact on the reader, acknowledged.
- What is being done to repair it and to prevent it.

## Materials that help
What exactly happened and when; what it cost the reader; what the user has already done; what they can commit to; the reader's message, if they complained.

## Diagnosis questions
- What exactly was the user's part, and what wasn't?
- What did it cost the reader?
- What is already fixed, and what can the user commit to, with a date?
- Should this be said in person first, with the email as the record?
- Is there anything legal or contractual that limits what can be admitted? (If so, flag it; the skill doesn't give legal advice.)

## How to phrase the message
What went wrong + the user's part + the repair.
- Weak: "Apologies for any inconvenience."
- Strong: "I sent the report with last quarter's numbers, and you presented them to the board; I've sent the corrected version to everyone on the list and added a second check before anything goes out."

## Typical structures
- **Own and repair:** the apology with what went wrong → the impact on the reader → what was done → what changes → an offer to talk.
- **Apology with a correction:** the apology → the corrected fact or deliverable → what changes.

## Pitfalls
- "If anyone was offended", "mistakes were made", "any inconvenience": apologies that avoid owning anything.
- Explaining the causes at length, which reads as excuses.
- Apologizing for more than the user did, to end the discomfort.
- Promising what can't be guaranteed ("this will never happen again").
- Asking the reader to reassure the user.
