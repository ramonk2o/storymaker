# Case: an explanation

Explaining something complex or easy to misread: why a decision was made, why a problem happened, how a change works, why the numbers are what they are. The reader wants **the answer to their question first, then only as much of the how and why as they need to act or trust it**.

## What the reader needs to leave with
- The answer, in one or two sentences, in their terms.
- The few facts that make it credible.
- What it means for them, and what to do, if anything.

## Materials that help
The question as the reader asked it; the facts, numbers and documents behind the answer; what the reader already knows; what they might suspect (that something is being hidden, that it's someone's fault).

## Diagnosis questions
- What did the reader actually ask, or what will they be wondering?
- What do they already know, and what words do they use for it?
- What will they do with the answer?
- What might they suspect, and should the email address it head-on?
- What detail can go in an attachment or a link instead of the body?

## How to phrase the message
The answer + why it should be trusted.
- Weak: "Some context on the drop in sales."
- Strong: "Sales fell 12% in June because the promotion moved from June to July this year; comparing June and July together, we're up 4% on last year."

## Typical structures
- **Answer first:** the answer → the two or three facts behind it → what it means for the reader → where the detail is.
- **Step by step:** the outcome → how it works, in numbered steps the reader will follow → what to do if something goes wrong.
- **Correcting a misreading:** what the reader understood → what is actually the case → why the difference matters.

## Pitfalls
- Starting with the history instead of the answer.
- Jargon the reader doesn't use.
- Explaining everything the writer knows instead of what the reader needs.
- A defensive tone that makes a neutral explanation sound like an excuse.
