# Case: argumentative essay

A long text that defends a position with reasons and evidence, for a publication, the author's own site or a general reader. Longer and more patient than a column: it has room to build the case, define terms and give the objection its full weight. The reader wants to know **what the author claims, whether the reasons hold, and what changes if they accept it**.

## What the reader needs to leave with
- The position, in one sentence they could repeat or argue with.
- The two or three reasons that carry it, each with evidence.
- The strongest objection, stated fairly, and why the position survives it.
- What follows: what they would see, think or do differently.

## Materials that help
Notes or a draft; the sources the argument rests on; texts that hold the opposite view; one or two texts of the author's, or by someone whose tone they want.

## Diagnosis questions
- What do you claim that a reasonable reader might reject?
- What made you think so: something you saw, read or lived?
- Which reason convinces you most, and which is weakest?
- Who holds the opposite view, and what is their best argument?
- Which terms does the argument depend on that readers may understand differently?
- What should change for the reader if you're right?

## How to phrase the central idea
Position + main reason + what follows.
- Weak: "Productivity culture has its problems."
- Strong: "We measure productivity by visible effort because output is hard to see, and that rewards the busiest people over the most useful; teams should judge by what shipped, not by who stayed late."

## Recommended arcs
- **Thesis first** (default), with tension kept inside each reason.
- **Situation, complication, resolution**, when the reader must first see why the usual view fails.

## Typical skeleton
1. Opening: a case or fact that makes the problem visible
2. The position, early
3. Terms defined, if the argument depends on them
4. Reason 1, with evidence
5. Reason 2, with evidence
6. The objection at full weight, and the answer
7. What follows for the reader
8. Close: back to the opening, seen differently

## Specific stress test
- Which reason would fall if one source turned out wrong?
- Is the objection the one opponents actually make, or an easier one?
- Does the conclusion go further than the reasons support?

## Pitfalls
- "On one hand, on the other" with no position.
- The position arriving in the last paragraph.
- A straw objection, or none.
- Citations crowding out the author's own reasoning.
- A close that only restates.

## Sample passage for the plan
The opening and the paragraph that states the position, where the reader decides whether the argument is worth following.
