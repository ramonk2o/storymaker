# Arcs

Used in step 2.1 and in the light proposal. The arc is the shape the argument takes through the text; the outline fills it paragraph by paragraph.

## First decision: answer first or build to it

Decide it explicitly with the user, from the reader and the kind of text.

- **Answer first** when the reader came for a position or a takeaway and can leave at any line: columns, most blog posts, a reader short on time. The position arrives in the first paragraphs; the rest earns it.
- **Build to it** when the reader has to walk the road to accept where it ends, or the value is the thinking itself: reflective and personal essays, a conclusion the reader would reject if stated cold. The text opens a question or a scene and resolves it toward the end.
- **Mixed:** the position stated early, with tension kept inside the body (each movement opens a question and answers it).

Whatever the choice, the reader always knows early why the text is worth their time.

## Arcs

Propose two or three that fit, with one line on what each does for this text, and recommend one.

### Thesis first
**Structure:** the position → the reasons, each with its evidence → the strongest objection and the answer → what follows for the reader.
**Use:** columns, argumentative essays, takes. **Avoid:** when the reader would reject the position stated cold.

### Situation, complication, resolution
**Structure:** something the reader accepts → what changed or doesn't fit → the question it raises → the answer, which is the central idea.
**Use:** explaining why something must be seen differently. **Avoid:** re-explaining what everyone knows; start at the complication.

### The question that resolves
**Structure:** a question that matters to the reader → why the usual answers fall short → the author's path through it → where they land, with honest uncertainty.
**Use:** reflective essays. **Avoid:** when the author has no landing; the text must still arrive somewhere.

### Scene to idea
**Structure:** a concrete scene → what it revealed → widening from the scene to what is true beyond it → a return to the scene, seen differently.
**Use:** personal essays, columns that open with a case. **Avoid:** a scene that only decorates; it has to carry the idea.

### Braid
**Structure:** two threads alternate (a story and an argument, a past and a present) and meet at the turn.
**Use:** long personal or reflective essays. **Avoid:** short texts; the threads need room to meet.

### Lesson learned
**Structure:** what we tried → what happened → why → what to do instead.
**Use:** blog posts from experience. **Avoid:** when nothing went differently than expected; then it is a how-to.

### How-to
**Structure:** the result first → the steps → the pitfalls → when not to do this.
**Use:** practical blog posts. **Avoid:** when the reader first needs to be convinced the result is worth it.

### What is vs. what could be
**Structure:** the current state → a turn that opens the gap → alternating contrast between now and possible → what it takes to get there.
**Use:** vision pieces, calls to change. **Avoid:** analytical texts where the reader wants evidence, not contrast.

## Opening, turn and close

- **Opening:** creates a tension or a promise that only the central idea resolves: a scene, a fact that contradicts what the reader believes, the question they already bring, the claim itself. A clever opening that leads nowhere wastes the reader's first decision.
- **Turn:** the paragraph where the arc changes direction and the central idea becomes visible. It is the passage the reader should remember; plan it, don't hope for it.
- **Close:** adds something the middle didn't give (what follows, what changed, the open question that remains) and, when it fits, returns to the opening with something changed. Never a summary.

## Checks on any arc

- **The reader leaves with something** they could repeat, use or argue with.
- **Objection with its full weight.** In any text that argues, the strongest objection gets a paragraph and a real answer; a straw objection persuades less than none.
- **The middle varies.** Long runs of paragraphs of the same kind (reasons, examples) lose the reader; alternate claim and case, abstract and concrete.
- **Seductive details are cut.** An anecdote, quote or fact that is interesting but doesn't serve the central idea lowers understanding; cut it or keep it for another text.
