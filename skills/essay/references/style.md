# Voice

Used in the Opening (voice source) and in step 2.3. A voice is settled as observable traits the user can check on the sample passage, never as adjectives alone ("elegant", "light", "punchy"), because adjectives mean different things to the user and to the model.

## Sources of a voice

The user can set the tone in any of these ways, alone or combined. Recommend one from the reader and the occasion, and accept whatever the user brings.

- **A register:** formal, casual, essayistic, technical, conversational. Derive the traits from the reader and the occasion.
- **The user's own voice:** read one or two texts they wrote and like, and describe what they actually do, not what they say they do.
- **Reference texts:** texts the user brings, by anyone (an article, a newsletter, a blog post, a passage of a book), whose tone they want. Read them and describe the traits they share; where they disagree with each other, ask which one wins. The texts ground the traits; they are never a template to copy.
- **A brand's voice guide:** turn the guide's adjectives into traits and check them on the brand's best recent texts.
- **A named person to take inspiration from:** a writer, a columnist, someone the user admires. See below.
- **Combinations:** "my own voice, but drier, like X" means the user's texts set the base and the named person adjusts one or two traits; say which trait comes from where.

## Traits to settle

Four to seven traits, each written as "does X, not Y" so it can be checked on the sample. Pick the ones that make this voice different from a neutral text; skip the rest.

1. **Sentence length and rhythm** — for example, "mostly 10 to 20 words; one short sentence to land a point".
2. **Paragraphs** — length, how they open, whether one-sentence paragraphs are allowed.
3. **Person and distance** — I, we, you or impersonal; whether the text addresses the reader.
4. **Vocabulary** — plain or learned, the jargon allowed, contractions.
5. **Figures and examples** — how often metaphors appear and where they come from; a concrete example per claim or not; humor or irony.
6. **How the argument moves** — states the position early or builds to it; how it concedes; how certain claims sound.
7. **Never does** — two to four explicit bans.

## Taking inspiration from a named person

- Describe the person's writing as traits, stated as your reading ("my reading of X: …"), and mark the ones you are unsure of. Your idea of a writer can be thin or wrong, and the user may not know the writer well enough to judge it; ask for a passage they like, because it grounds the traits in something both of you can see. If the person isn't a writer you know, or the user names someone with little published text, ask for samples instead of guessing.
- Imitate traits, never passages: write new sentences that share the person's rhythm, distance and choices, and don't reproduce or closely paraphrase their text.
- Leave out signature phrases, catchphrases and recurring images; they turn the text into a caricature. A device the person is known for (addressing the reader, say) can be a trait if used sparingly; a phrase can't.
- Don't reuse the sentences, images or structure of any passage the user brought. Before the style check, compare the text with those passages yourself and rewrite anything that echoes them, since the style check never sees them.
- Say which traits carry over to the language of the text and which don't, when it differs from the person's.
- Never present the text as the person's, and never attribute to them a quote you can't source.

## Pitfalls
- Adjectives standing in for traits ("fluid", "engaging").
- Pastiche: every sentence showing every trait at once.
- The model's own habits slipping in as if they were part of the voice.
- A voice that works against what the reader needs. When they conflict, the reader wins.
