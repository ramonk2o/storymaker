# Outline

Used in step 2.2, in the light proposal, in the Opening when reworking a draft, and in Phase 3 to compare the written text with the approved outline. The outline is the text read from above: one line per paragraph, so the user can judge the order, the weight and the gaps of the argument before any prose exists.

## The line

```
I. [Movement] — its job in the arc
  §1 [The paragraph's claim] — proof or material — ~[words] — [mark]
```

- **A claim, not a topic.** "Juniors pay most for remote work", not "Juniors". Up to about 12 words, a full sentence. A line that needs an "and" is probably two paragraphs.
- **Proof or material:** what the paragraph uses to make its claim (a scene, a case, a number with its comparison, a quote with its source), or "argument" when it reasons from what came before. A claim with nothing next to it is a gap: find the proof, mark it `[TO CONFIRM]`, or cut the line.
- **Words:** an estimate per paragraph, so the proportion is visible. The movement that carries the central idea usually gets the most words; a long setup with a short payoff shows up here before it shows up in the prose.
- **Mark:** `opening`, `turn` (the point where the arc changes direction), `objection`, `close`. Most lines have none.
- **Movements:** the parts of the arc (`references/arcs.md`), each with its job in one line. Headings in the published text follow the case file, not the movements.

## Checks before showing it

- **Sequence test.** Read only the lines, in order: they must tell the whole argument. Between two consecutive lines you should be able to say "so", "but" or "because"; where only "and also" fits, the order is a list, not an argument, and the lines are regrouped or reordered.
- **Cut test.** For each line, what is lost without it? A line kept because it is interesting, or because it took work to find, goes.
- **Opening and close.** The first line does the job the arc gives it, with no warm-up. The last line adds something the middle didn't give; a close that restates is cut.
- **Objection.** The objection's line sits where the reader would raise it, not at the end as an afterthought.
- **Length.** The words add up to the planned length, within about 10%.

## Long texts

Above about 2,500 words, outline in two passes: first the movements, with their job and word budget, for the user to react to; then the paragraphs inside them. Both go into the gate together.

## Showing it

The outline is text in the gate document. When the text is long and the session can draw inline, it may also be shown as a strip of blocks sized by words, one per paragraph and grouped by movement, so the user sees weight and rhythm at a glance; offer this in one line, don't make it the default.

## Reverse outline

The same format applied to an existing text, used in two places:

- **Reworking a draft (Opening).** Write one line per paragraph of the draft with what it actually claims, not what it seems to want to claim. Then flag: paragraphs with no claim, paragraphs with two, claims that repeat, claims out of order (one that depends on something said later), the step missing to reach the conclusion, and where the proportion is off. Propose the new order as an outline, marking what moved, what was cut and what is new.
- **Checking the written text (Phase 3).** The cold reader outlines the finished text back without seeing the plan. Compare its lines with the approved ones: a paragraph whose reverse line doesn't match its planned line drifted in the writing. Fix the paragraph, not the outline, unless the user approves changing the plan.
