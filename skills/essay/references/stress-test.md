# Stress test

Used in step 1.2 and in the light proposal. The goal is to press the central idea until it can stand up to a reader who disagrees: better for the text to take the beating here than in the comments.

## How to run it
- Use the question-round format from `SKILL.md`.
- **Open with the pre-mortem.** "The text is published and it didn't land: readers dropped it, misread it or pushed back. Why?" Recommend the two or three most likely reasons for this text and reader, each with how the plan answers it. Imagining a failure that already happened surfaces risks a plain critique misses.
- **Ground each question in this text.** Find the weak spot in the material (a claim with no source, a single example presented as a pattern, a number with no comparison) and recommend how to defend or fix it. A generic question gets a rubber-stamp answer.
- **Follow the tree.** The central idea first; only once it is firm, the other branches. Usually one or two rounds are enough; in the light path, a single round.
- The bank below is a menu, not a script. Add the "Specific stress test" questions from the case file.

Every objection raised is resolved: it changes the central idea or a supporting point, becomes the objection paragraph, or is left out on purpose and the user is told so.

## Question bank by branch
- **Central idea:** Could a reasonable person disagree? What does the reader gain or lose by accepting it? Is it said elsewhere already, and what does this text add? If the text had one sentence, which would it be?
- **Reader:** What do they think today? What would make them stop reading? What do they need to know that the text assumes?
- **Evidence:** Where does each fact come from? Which is the most fragile? Is one case standing in for a pattern? Was anything that contradicts the idea left out, and why?
- **Author:** Why is this author the one to say it? What did they see that others didn't? Which part of the view are they least sure of, and does the text hedge there and only there?
- **Cut:** What is the weakest paragraph, and what is lost without it? Is anything in because it took work to find? If the text had to be half as long, what goes first?
- **Close:** What should the reader think or do after the last line? Does the close give them that, or only repeat the middle?
